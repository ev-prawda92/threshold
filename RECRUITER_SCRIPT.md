# Five recruiters, thirty minutes each

The whole product rests on one behaviour: a recruiter clicking a specific
requirement at the moment they reject someone. No amount of building tests that.
Five conversations will.

## Before the call

Send the shareable link the day before with one line: *"Built a thing about why
reqs stall — takes two minutes, would love thirty seconds of your reaction."*
People who arrive having clicked it give better answers than people watching you
demo.

Do not send a deck. Do not explain the thesis first — you will contaminate every
answer you get.

## Who to ask

- **In-house technical recruiters** — the actual user. Most important.
- **TA leaders / heads of recruiting** — the buyer. Different answers; ask both.
- **Agency recruiters** — fastest to reach, and their incentives are different
  enough that a "yes" from them means less. Use them for practice runs.

You are talking to recruiters right now as a candidate. At the end of a screen,
"can I show you something I built and get your reaction" is an easy ask from
someone they are already invested in. That is five conversations without a
single cold email.

## The five questions, in order

Ask them **before** showing anything. The demo goes in the middle, at question 4.

**1. Walk me through the last person you rejected at screen. What did you
actually click?**
Past tense, one specific person. Never "what do you usually do" — that gets you
the process as designed, not the process as run. Shut up and let them narrate.

**2. Who ever looks at those reasons?**
If the answer is "nobody," the data is decoration and you are proposing to make
it matter. If the answer is "our TA analytics dashboard," find out what it
actually shows — it is almost always stage conversion, not attribution.

**3. When a req has been open forty days, how do you work out why?**
Listen for whether they can. Most people describe a meeting, a gut feeling about
the market, and a hiring manager who thinks the candidates are weak. That gap is
the product.

**4. [Now share the link.] Work through two or three. Then: would you have
clicked one of those, or would you have hit "other"?**
Say nothing while they click. The silence is the data.

**5. What is wrong with this? What would make it useless to you?**
Ask it exactly that way. "What do you think" gets politeness; "what would make
it useless" gets the truth.

## What to watch, not just hear

- **Do they hit "other"?** Once is fine. Every time means the gates you parsed
  are not the criteria they use, and the parser is the problem.
- **Do they reject with nothing selected?** That is the failure mode that kills
  the product. Count it.
- **Do they correct the pre-check?** Good — it means they read it. If they never
  correct it, they may be clicking through, which is worse than a blank field
  because it produces confident wrong data.
- **How long per candidate?** If it is materially slower than what they do
  today, nothing else matters.
- **Do they scroll the ledger, or go straight to the buttons?** Tells you whether
  the scorecard is a product or decoration.

## Reading the result

**Go** — most of them attribute to a specific gate without prompting, "other"
stays under a third, and at least one says a version of *"I could actually use
that at the end of a screen."* Then configure per-job rejection reasons in one
willing company's ATS and run it live for sixty days.

**Change shape** — they attribute fine but nobody cares about the audit. The
value is elsewhere: rerouting, or the candidate side, or the compliance artifact.
Follow whichever one they got animated about.

**Stop** — they cannot or will not attribute, or they already do and nothing
comes of it. That is a real answer and it cost you a week rather than a year.
Write it down and move on.

## Housekeeping

Ask before recording. Take notes in their words, not your summary of their
words — "I just pick whatever gets the box off my screen" is worth more than
"low engagement with rejection reasons." Send a two-line thank you the same day
and tell them what you changed because of what they said; that is what gets you
the second conversation and the introduction to their colleague.

## What not to do

Do not defend the product. Do not explain what they *should* have clicked. Do
not ask "would you pay for this" — everyone says maybe, and it tells you nothing
next to watching where their cursor goes.
